RAG, or How a Machine Finds the Right Page

Learn why sending an entire document is not scalable, and preview embeddings, chunking, vector search and reranking before the concepts class.

About 35–40 minutesRead before classQuick checks included

1. Welcome

The website summariser sends a whole page to the model. That crude approach fails when the source is a handbook, textbook or years of tickets. RAG asks how to find the few relevant pieces instead.

What to focus on
Understand the mental model first; you do not need to memorise every detail before class.

2. Why RAG exists

Retrieval-Augmented Generation retrieves relevant source material before the model answers. The passages are added to the prompt so answers can be grounded in current or private information. RAG can avoid retraining when documents change and can provide sources to check.

3. The two phases

RAG has a preparation/indexing phase and a query-time phase. During preparation, documents are split into chunks and represented for search, often as embeddings stored in a vector database. At query time, the question is embedded, relevant chunks are retrieved and the model answers from them.

4. Embeddings: meaning as coordinates

An embedding maps text to a vector of numbers. Semantically similar text can have nearby vectors, enabling search beyond exact keyword matches. Embeddings are representations, not a complete retrieval system by themselves.

5. Chunking changes what you can find

A chunk is a piece of a larger document. Large chunks may include irrelevant material; small chunks may lose context. Overlap can preserve continuity but increases storage and duplicate retrieval. Chunk boundaries should respect source structure when possible.

6. Vector databases and retrieval quality

A vector database stores vectors and metadata and searches for nearby vectors. It does not guarantee that the right answer was retrieved. Reranking reorders candidates using a stronger relevance model; hybrid search combines semantic similarity with keyword matching.
